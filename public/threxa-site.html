14–15% is the figure the trade press reports for the average Indian corrugation plant. Most owners we meet have never measured their own.

The problem

Your accounts are current.
Your floor isn't.

Accounting software closes your books. It cannot tell you which machine is idle, how much material went to scrap on a particular job, or whether today's dispatch actually left the gate. That information lives in registers, in WhatsApp groups, and in the supervisor's head.

// 01
Wastage found too late

Material issued against material consumed gets reconciled at stock-take, months after the job ran. By then nobody remembers which machine, which operator or which order caused it, and the loss is absorbed into cost without ever being questioned.

// 02
A floor you can't see

Which machine is running, which is idle, which order is behind — answering that means walking the floor or calling a supervisor. By the time a delay reaches the office, the customer has usually noticed it first.

// 03
Numbers that disagree

Production register, stock register, dispatch book and accounts each tell a slightly different story. Month-end becomes an argument about which one is correct instead of a decision about what to do next.

Why it's built this way

Built on a shop floor,
not in an office.

Threxa was built first for corrugated box manufacturing — an industry where a single percent of trim loss is the whole margin. That meant modelling reel stock by GSM and BF, job cards that follow corrugation through printing, die cutting and pasting, and quotations calculated from board grade and box dimensions.

Most of that depth is invisible to a plastics or garment unit. What is not invisible is the consequence: a system designed by someone who stood on a factory floor asks for the numbers a business actually has, in the order the work actually happens. That is the difference between software a supervisor uses and software a supervisor works around.

It runs in a browser. No server, no database licence, no IT staff.
